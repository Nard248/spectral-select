\begin{abstract}
Selecting a few excitation--emission pairs from a multi-excitation fluorescence image is a fixed-budget combinatorial problem in which the value of a pair depends on the pairs already chosen. Constructive reinforcement-learning (RL) policies can only add, so early mistakes are permanent. We formulate pair selection as a reversible Markov decision process with add, remove and stop actions, paired credit for each remove--add swap, tabu masking, and a permutation-invariant candidate scorer built on label-free residual-variance features, so that one policy applies to datasets with different candidate sets. On four annotated datasets (two lichen, two collagen) with nested, specimen-grouped evaluation we separate three regimes. On a single labelled dataset RL is a weak black-box optimiser: simulated annealing and a genetic algorithm reach (near-)exact optima at $n=3$ and higher objective values at a matched budget. Deployed \emph{without labels} on an unseen sample type, the reversible policy beats a matched add-only policy by $+0.025$ balanced accuracy (pooled over targets, sources and budgets; 95\% CI $[0.022,0.028]$; significant for all four variants), the gain is largest across sample families, and the reversible policy is not harder to train. A label-free greedy column-subset baseline (CSS) is nevertheless strong at small budgets, though below random selection at $n=13$ on three of four targets; using the swap policy to refine the CSS set helps on Lichens 1, lowers accuracy on Lichens 2 and leaves it unchanged on both collagen targets. With 25 labelled evaluations, transferred policies match simulated annealing, which overtakes them at larger budgets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Multi-excitation fluorescence hyperspectral imaging records, for every pixel, an excitation--emission matrix (EEM): the sample is illuminated at several excitation wavelengths $\lambda^{\mathrm{ex}}$ and a full emission spectrum is recorded at each~\cite{lakowicz2006}. One measurement channel is therefore a \emph{pair} $c=(\lambda^{\mathrm{ex}},\lambda^{\mathrm{em}})$, and an acquisition yields 149--210 admissible pairs after the bands nearest to the excitation wavelength and to twice the excitation wavelength (first- and second-order scattered excitation light) are removed. Measuring all of them is slow, photobleaches the sample and needs a switchable multi-LED source with a tunable emission filter; a handful of well-chosen pairs would allow a cheap instrument with a few LEDs and fixed filters. Choosing $n$ of $M$ pairs is a combinatorial problem ($\binom{192}{13}\approx5\times10^{19}$), and the value of a pair depends on the pairs already chosen.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is attractive for such sequential set construction~\cite{bello2016,khalil2017,kool2019}, and has been applied to hyperspectral band selection~\cite{mou2022drl}. Constructive policies, however, can only \emph{add}: an early mistake is permanent. In combinatorial RL, reversible actions that also remove elements improved solution quality~\cite{barrett2020eco,chen2019rewriting}. We ask whether the same holds for spectral pair selection, and, crucially, \emph{where} RL can add value at all. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a reversible add/remove/stop MDP for fixed-budget pair selection with paired credit assignment, tabu masking and a permutation-invariant candidate scorer that transfers across datasets with different candidate sets;
\item a protocol that separates three regimes---per-instance search at a matched evaluation budget, label-free transfer to an unseen dataset, and few-shot search with a small labelled budget---with nested, specimen-grouped evaluation;
\item a study on four labelled fluorescence datasets (two lichen, two collagen) against classic search (greedy, swap, beam, simulated annealing, genetic), exact small-budget optima and label-free baselines.
\end{itemize}
We find that reversibility helps in label-free transfer to new data (S2); with a few labelled evaluations on the target, look-ahead over best-of-$k$ sampling brings no significant gain when pooled over datasets and budgets (S3), and classic search remains the better optimiser of a fixed objective.

\section{Problem}
Let $\mathcal{C}=\{c_j\}_{j=1}^{M}$ be the admissible pairs (bands near $\lambda^{\mathrm{ex}}$ and $2\lambda^{\mathrm{ex}}$ removed). Pixels $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^M$ carry a class $y_i$ and a specimen id $g_i$. For a budget $n$ we seek
\begin{equation}
S^\star=\arg\max_{S\subset\mathcal{C},\,|S|=n} \mathrm{BA}_{\mathrm{test}}(S),
\end{equation}
the balanced accuracy of a classifier trained on the selected pairs and tested on \emph{specimens not used for selection}. The search can only use a surrogate computed on training specimens. We use two:
\textbf{Supervised} $\JS(S)$: the balanced accuracy of shrinkage LDA under inner whole-specimen cross-validation. Per inner fold we precompute class sums and the pooled within-class scatter for all $M$ pairs once; scoring any $S$ is then a $|S|\times|S|$ submatrix solve ($\approx0.5$\,ms), which makes RL training with $10^5$--$10^6$ evaluations feasible.
\textbf{Label-free} $\JR(S)=\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{:S}\Sigma_{SS}^{-1}\Sigma_{S:})/\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma$: the fraction of variance of all $M$ standardised pairs that is linearly predictable from $S$ (column-subset selection~\cite{boutsidis2009css}), computable on a new, unlabelled sample.
Labels are expensive and absent for new samples, so a method meant for new data must act label-free; $\JS$ is available only on annotated training datasets.

%=====================================================================
\section{Reversible selection MDP}
\textbf{State and actions.} The state is the current set $S_t$, a phase $q_t\in\{\add,\rem\}$ and a tabu list. Episodes start from $S_0=\emptyset$ (or from a label-free greedy set) and \emph{construct} by \add{} until $|S|=n$. In \emph{add/remove} mode they then run up to $T=n$ refinement cycles: the agent either \stp{}s or removes a pair $c_r\in S$, after which only an \add{} of $c_a\notin S$ is allowed, so every cycle is a learned swap $S'=(S\setminus\{c_r\})\cup\{c_a\}$ that preserves the budget. Masks forbid duplicates, invalid pairs, re-adding a removed pair for $L=3$ cycles and removing the pair just added; revisiting a subset costs $-0.01$.
\textbf{Reward.} Construction steps receive $r_t=J(S_{t+1})-J(S_t)$ with $J(\emptyset)$ at chance. A removal receives $0$ and the following addition $J(S')-J(S)$, so the removal is credited only through the return of the completed swap ($\gamma=1$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$), not by an explicit split. Without the revisit penalty the return telescopes to $J(S_{\mathrm{final}})-J(S_0)$, with $S_0=\emptyset$ ($J(\emptyset)$ at chance) for construction episodes and $S_0$ the CSS set for CSS-start episodes; each revisit lowers it by $0.01$.
\textbf{Candidate features.} Each pair $c$ is described, given $S$, by label-free quantities standardised within the task: wavelengths and Stokes shift (set to zero in the wavelength-free configuration used for all S2 and S3 results; S1 and the dashed curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:training}a use the full set), log-intensity mean and spread, residual variance of $c$ given $S$, the gain in $\JR$ (and in a whitened-PCA variant) if $c$ is added or the loss if it is removed---all from rank-one updates of the residual covariance---maximum and mean $|\mathrm{corr}|$ with $S$, and excitation-sharing indicators. Optional supervised features (Fisher score, exact pair-enumeration priors) are used only when labels are available.
\textbf{Policy.} A shared MLP embeds every candidate; the set context is the mean and max of the selected embeddings plus global features (phase, $|S|/n$, current $\JR$)~\cite{zaheer2017deepsets}. Separate \add{} and \rem{} heads score candidates, a \stp{} head and a value head read the context; logits are masked. The policy ($\approx$38k parameters) never sees a pair index, so it applies to any candidate set. We train with PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} on batches of 64 episodes sampled over tasks and budgets $n\in\{3,5,8,13\}$, masking a random 20\% of pairs as augmentation; REINFORCE~\cite{williams1992} is an ablation.
\textbf{Deployment.} Label-free: a greedy rollout, or the best of 16 sampled rollouts ranked by $\JR$; no labels and no $\JS$ calls, and the \emph{final} set is returned. Few-shot ($\JS$ observable, budget $B$): best-of-$k$ sampling for add-only, and Monte-Carlo look-ahead for add/remove, which evaluates the top removals with sampled replacements and applies the best improving swap.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\textbf{Data.} Four annotated datasets from one system (WheeLED WLS-23-A switchable LED source, Nuance FX tunable-filter camera, emission 420--720\,nm in 10\,nm steps). \emph{Lichens 1} (8 excitations 310--430\,nm, $M=192$) is columns 466--1390 of the palette in~\cite{lichens_zenodo}: lower and upper surface of \emph{Ramalina sinensis}, \emph{Flavopunctelia soredica} and \emph{Ramalina fraxinea}, four pieces each. \emph{Lichens 2} (8 excitations, $M=210$) is a separate, undeposited acquisition of 28 pieces in five annotated classes whose taxa are not recorded \OPEN{taxa; shared thalli with Lichens 1?}. \emph{Collagen-AA} and \emph{Collagen-P} (6 excitations 310--400\,nm, $M=149$ and 158~\cite{collagen_zenodo}) are nine collagen sponges each (acetic-acid or pepsin extraction) with three crosslinker levels as classes. $M$ counts the pairs left after removing bands within 40\,nm (Collagen-P: 30\,nm) of $\lambda^{\mathrm{ex}}$ and $2\lambda^{\mathrm{ex}}$. A specimen is a connected annotated region, one physical piece except in the fifth Lichens 2 class (two fragments of one piece); it is the unit of independence. The processed files label the emission axis of a few excitations 15--40\,nm off the recorded axis; this changes no feature or selected set, only the wavelength-position features (S1 and the with-wavelength S2 variants) and the axis of Fig.~\ref{fig:eem}, which we plot on recorded wavelengths.
\textbf{Nested, specimen-grouped evaluation.} Below, the datasets are called Lichens 1, Lichens 2, Collagen-AA and Collagen-P. Outer folds hold out at least one whole specimen per class ($k=4,2,3,3$ folds; three repeats, i.e.\ re-drawn outer splits; S1 and S3 use repeat 0, S2 all three). A \emph{seed} is the randomness of a search run or of policy training: 3 for the classical wrappers, 1 for per-instance RL, 2 for the non-augmented transferred policies~\cite{roberts2017cv,varma2006bias}. Every selection method sees only the training specimens; $\JS$ uses inner whole-specimen folds on a per-specimen capped subsample ($\approx$3000 px). Selected sets are scored on the held-out specimens by kNN ($k=5$, primary, as in earlier work on these data), shrinkage LDA, an RBF-SVM and a random forest; we report balanced accuracy (BA) and macro-F1, the number of distinct excitations, mean $|\mathrm{corr}|$, and selection stability (Kuncheva index~\cite{kuncheva2007stability}).
\textbf{Methods.} Label-free: random (10 draws), top variance, a uniform excitation--emission grid, SPA~\cite{araujo2001spa}, greedy $\JR$ (column-subset greedy, CSS) and its whitened-PCA variant, and a variance-relevance/correlation-penalty rule mimicking an earlier selector for these data. Supervised filters: Fisher score and mRMR~\cite{peng2005mrmr}. Wrapper search on $\JS$ with a budget of $B$ uncached $\JS$ evaluations (every scored set counts, partial sets included): random search, sequential forward selection (SFS), SFS followed by best-improvement swaps, beam search (width 5) with swaps, simulated annealing (SA)~\cite{kirkpatrick1983sa}, a steady-state genetic algorithm (GA), and exact enumeration for $n\le3$. If the budget ends before any complete $n$-set has been scored (SFS+swap when $B$ is below one forward step, $\approx M$ evaluations; Beam+swap at $n=13$), the best partial set is completed with the label-free CSS rule and its $\JS$ is computed outside the budget.
\textbf{Regimes.} \emph{S1 search:} all wrappers and per-instance RL on each training task, $B=5000$, best-so-far set. Every step of an RL rollout scores a set, so 5000 evaluations correspond to only 25--91 (add-only) and 14--42 (add/remove) PPO updates of 16 episodes on average; the 250-update runs of S2 instead train one policy on many tasks with no per-instance budget. \emph{S2 transfer:} policies trained with $\JS$ reward on the source datasets---leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) and cross-family (Lichens$\rightarrow$Collagen, Collagen$\rightarrow$Lichens)---and deployed label-free on every outer fold of the target. \emph{S3 few-shot:} the LODO policy with $\JS$ observable on the target and $B\in\{25,\dots,400\}$. Under LODO the source pool of each lichen target contains the other lichen dataset, and that of each collagen target the other collagen dataset (same sponge design, same session), so S3 and the LODO results of S2 are closer to in-domain than the cross-family results. Budgets $n\in\{3,5,8,13\}$ throughout.

\textbf{Per-instance search (S1, Table~\ref{tab:search}).} On a single labelled dataset RL is a black-box optimiser, and a weak one: at $B=5000$ evaluations SA and GA reach the highest inner $\JS$ (0.920/0.924 at $n=13$ versus 0.883/0.887 for add-only/add-remove RL; add-only RL is even below random search, 0.885) and reach the exact optimum at $n=3$ up to a mean gap below 0.001 (SA misses it in 5 and GA in 1 of 36 runs, by at most 0.005), while SFS+swap and beam search stop 0.008--0.009 short; RL was not run at $n=3$. RL needs $\approx$2900 evaluations to come within 0.005 of its own final value (Fig.~\ref{fig:training}b). Beam+swap at $n=13$ ($\JS$ 0.845) is budget truncation, not a search result: its first level costs $M$ evaluations and each further level up to $5M$, so 5000 evaluations do not reach depth 13; in all 12 tasks the beam stops early and 5.7 of the 13 pairs, on average, are added by CSS. Outer-test accuracy differences between all other searchers are small (0.84--0.86 at $n=13$) because the inner objective is optimistic: its gap to the outer BA is 0.04--0.06 (0.026 for the truncated Beam+swap) and grows with search strength (SA/GA 0.064 at $n=13$, random search 0.046), the winner's curse of selecting on few specimens~\cite{varma2006bias}. Among RL design choices PPO is clearly better than REINFORCE (0.870 vs 0.839 inner $\JS$), exact pair-enumeration priors do not repay their 11k-evaluation cost, terminal and shaped rewards end at the same value, and a policy trained with half the budget ($10^4$ evaluations) followed by Monte-Carlo look-ahead (about 160 further evaluations) gives the highest outer accuracy on Collagen-AA (0.779 vs 0.767 for SA with the full $B=20000$; 3 folds and one seed, so within noise).

\textbf{Label-free transfer (S2, Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}, Table~\ref{tab:transfer}).} This is the regime the reversible action was meant for, and the add/remove policy is consistently better than the matched add-only policy: pooled over targets, sources and budgets $n\in\{3,5,8,13\}$ the gain is $+0.025$ BA (95\% CI $[0.022, 0.028]$, 837 wins/315 losses over 1152 paired units, i.e.\ four variants $\times$ 288 target/source/$n$/repeat/fold units); on cell means (32 target$\times$source$\times n$ cells) it is $+0.017$ to $+0.031$ for each of the four policy/deployment variants (Holm $p\le0.008$, $\ge$21/32 cells). The gain is largest across sample families ($+0.024$ to $+0.045$ per variant over its 144 cross-family units, against $+0.013$ to $+0.017$ under LODO) where the constructive policy errs most. For the variant of Table~\ref{tab:transfer} (wavelength-free, best of 16, cross-family) it is $+0.024$ pooled, and $+0.017$ as the unweighted mean of the 12 tabulated cells ($n=5$ is not shown); on Lichens 1 it holds in all 12 units at every $n$ ($+0.027$ to $+0.062$ for this variant). Removing the wavelength-position features improves every policy ($+0.013$ to $+0.026$, $p<10^{-5}$): absolute or relative wavelengths encode the chemistry of the source and mislead on another sample type. In absolute terms, CSS is strong at $n=3$ (best label-free value on three of four targets) but not at $n=13$, where it is below random selection on three of four targets (Lichens 1 0.898 vs 0.948, Collagen-AA 0.655 vs 0.727, Collagen-P 0.721 vs 0.785), and at $n=8$ on Collagen-AA (0.688 vs 0.699). The transferred add/remove policy alone rarely beats CSS significantly: its mean exceeds CSS in 5 of the 12 cells of Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and significantly in one (Lichens 1, $n=13$, $+0.042$, 12/12 units), while it is significantly worse at Lichens 1, $n=3$ ($-0.021$). \textbf{Task augmentation and CSS refinement (S2).} To enlarge the small source pools we retrained every policy on augmented tasks (class subsets, excitation dropout; 42--105 tasks per pool, one seed; 250 PPO updates, as for the non-augmented policies, except 400 in the training logs of the cross-family add-only and add/remove ones). The reversible advantage replicates on these independently trained policies: add/remove beats add-only by $+0.019$ cross-family (86/58 units, unit-level Wilcoxon $p=0.005$; $+0.023$, $p=10^{-7}$ with best-of-16) and by $+0.027$ under LODO (114/30, $p=10^{-13}$). Augmentation itself did not help: against the non-augmented policies it changes add/remove by $-0.014$ cross-family ($p=2\times10^{-4}$ greedy, $4\times10^{-6}$ best-of-16) and by $+0.002$/$-0.001$ under LODO (n.s.), and add-only by $+0.011$ (greedy)/$-0.013$ (best-of-16) cross-family; the unequal training length is a confound. Mixing the label-free objective into the training reward ($\Delta\JS+0.5\,\Delta\JR$) recovers $+0.013$ cross-family ($p=0.001$) but costs $0.007$ under LODO. Started from the CSS set (``+aug'', Table~\ref{tab:transfer}) the policy is a conservative refiner, not a reliable one: the collagen-trained policy edits it on the lichen targets, improving it on Lichens 1 (0.937$\rightarrow$0.946 at $n=8$, 11/12 units; 0.898$\rightarrow$0.963 at $n=13$, 12/12, above random at 0.948; but $-0.010$ at $n=5$, 5/12 units improved) and lowering it on Lichens 2 ($-0.029$ at $n=8$, $-0.017$ at $n=13$; $-0.053$ at $n=5$, not tabulated). The lichen-trained policy and every LODO policy stop at once and return CSS unchanged.

\textbf{Few-shot search (S3, Fig.~\ref{fig:fewshot}, Table~\ref{tab:fewshot}).} When a few labelled evaluations are allowed on the target, the transferred policy is a strong prior: at $B=25$ both RL variants match SA (differences to SA pooled over the four datasets, $n\in\{3,5,8,13\}$ and 48 fold units: $+0.007$ for add/remove with look-ahead and $0.000$ for add-only best-of-$k$, both n.s.). At $n=8$ (Table~\ref{tab:fewshot}) the add/remove policy with look-ahead has the best accuracy at $B=25$ and $100$ on Lichens 1, Lichens 2 and Collagen-AA and at $B=400$ on Collagen-AA (on Lichens 1 at $B=400$ it is within 0.001 of add-only). It is not generally better at the inner objective: averaged over the four datasets at $n=8$, SA has the higher inner $\JS$ than add/remove at $B=100$ (0.876 vs 0.849) for similar outer BA (0.836 vs 0.833), while at $B=25$ SA has 0.831 vs 0.824 inner but 0.794 vs 0.827 outer. Classic search catches up as the budget grows: at $B=400$ SA is ahead of the add/remove and add-only policies by 0.024 and 0.017 (same pooling as above; Holm $p=0.08$/$0.008$; 0.009 and 0.016 at $n=8$ alone), driven by Collagen-P, where GA and SA reach 0.920 and 0.908 and the policies plateau at 0.82--0.85. Look-ahead versus best-of-$k$ add-only differs by at most 0.008 pooled over datasets, $n$ and budgets (n.s.); at $n=8$ alone the difference is $+0.028$, $+0.030$ and $+0.007$ at $B=25$, 100, 400, concentrated on Lichens 2 ($+0.078$ at $B=25$). SFS+swap is a supervised reference, not an upper bound: for $B$ below one forward step ($\approx M$ evaluations) it fixes one pair by SFS and fills the other 7 with CSS ($B=25$, 100), 6 at $B=200$ and 5 at $B=400$, which is why its accuracy does not increase with $B$ (Collagen-AA 0.716$\rightarrow$0.688).

\textbf{Is the reversible policy harder to train?} No. Despite the longer episodes and the delayed credit of a swap, add/remove policies reach within 0.01 of their final training return (mean of the last 20 updates; first update at which the 10-update moving average gets there) after 108 PPO updates versus 143 for add-only without wavelength features, and 138 versus 153 with them (Fig.~\ref{fig:training}a), with a comparable final value (0.833 vs 0.824 without, 0.846 vs 0.839 with wavelength features). We did not ablate the tabu mask, the \stp{} action or the revisit penalty.

\textbf{Which pairs, and at what hardware cost?} Fig.~\ref{fig:eem} shows that label-free CSS returns almost the same few pairs in every fold, whereas the reversible policy and the supervised searchers spread their choices over more excitations, mostly 365--430\,nm, and over the short- and long-wavelength ends of the emission range. In a separate per-instance experiment on Lichens 1 ($n=8$, $B=5000$, 4 outer folds of repeat 0, one seed), adding a cost $\mu\in\{0,0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05\}$ per distinct excitation to $\JS$ reduces the mean number of light sources from 5.25 ($\mu=0$) to 1.00 ($\mu=0.05$) for the add/remove RL policy (4.75 to 1.50 for SA), while kNN BA is 0.970 at $\mu=0$ and 0.931 at $\mu=0.05$ for RL (SA: 0.970 and 0.915); four folds give no reliable intervals.



\section{Discussion and conclusion}
The study answers the question that motivated it with a qualified yes. The ability to remove and swap pairs makes a transferred selection policy consistently better than an otherwise identical add-only policy, by 2--3 BA points on average and up to 6 points, and the advantage is largest when the policy meets a sample type unlike its training data. This supports the intuition that undo actions matter most where a constructive policy is miscalibrated~\cite{barrett2020eco}. The reversible policy was not harder to train: it reached its plateau in fewer updates than the add-only policy without wavelength features and in about as many with them.

Three negative results are equally important for practice. First, when the selection objective can be evaluated cheaply on the target, RL is not the right tool: SA and GA find better subsets at the same budget, and they reach the certified optimum at $n=3$. Second, the inner objective overfits few specimens, so strong search buys little on held-out specimens; reporting the inner--outer gap should be standard. Third, a label-free greedy column-subset rule is a strong baseline at small budgets that most published band selectors do not report (though it is below random selection at $n=13$ on three of four targets), so a learned policy should be judged against it and against random selection. Using the policy to refine that greedy set is not reliably safe: it helped on Lichens 1, lowered accuracy on Lichens 2, and stopped immediately on the collagen targets. Wavelength-position features hurt transfer, which suggests that what transfers between sample types is a statistical selection rule, not a spectral prior.

\textbf{Limitations.} We had four datasets from two sample families and one instrument, so the source pools for transfer are small (two or three datasets); task augmentation by class subsets and excitation dropout only partly substitutes for more acquisitions. Specimen counts are low (three per class for collagen), which makes every outer estimate noisy. Simulation-based training, for example in a physics-based simulator, and sequential acquisition where the policy controls the instrument are natural next steps.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Reversible add/remove/stop actions are a simple, robust improvement for RL-based spectral pair selection on new data, but classic search should remain the default for a fixed labelled dataset, and a label-free greedy baseline should be reported alongside random selection. % TODO(authors): code-availability statement.
